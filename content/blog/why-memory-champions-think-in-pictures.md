It's easy to assume people who can memorize a shuffled deck of cards have some kind of photographic memory. The research tells a different, more encouraging story: memory champions mostly have ordinary brains, trained to use a few good habits.

## What brain scans found

In a well-known 2003 study published in *Nature Neuroscience*, researchers at University College London compared top memory competitors with ordinary volunteers. They found no meaningful difference in general intelligence or in brain structure. What *was* different was how they memorized. Nearly all of the champions used a spatial strategy, the [memory palace](/blog/build-your-first-memory-palace), and their brains showed more activity in regions involved in spatial memory and navigation.

In other words, they weren't remembering better because of what they had, but because of what they *did*.

## Ordinary people can learn it

A later study, published in the journal *Neuron* in 2017, took this a step further. Volunteers with no memory training practised the memory palace technique for about 30 minutes a day over six weeks. Their ability to memorize word lists more than doubled, and their brain connectivity patterns shifted to look more like those of the memory athletes. Months later, much of that improvement was still there.

That's the core idea behind this site: these are skills, and skills can be trained.

## Why pictures beat words

Every technique here, from [linking](/tactics/linking-method) to the [PAO system](/tactics/pao-system), does the same basic thing: it turns information your brain finds dull (words, numbers, names) into images your brain finds interesting.

Psychologists call one part of this the **picture superiority effect**: people remember pictures better than words. Memory techniques push it further by making those pictures:

- **Unusual.** We notice and keep what surprises us.
- **Active.** Movement and action are hard to ignore.
- **Emotional or funny.** Feelings help a memory stick.
- **Connected.** An image tied to a place or to another image has a way back in.

## How to start training

You don't need hours a day. Short, regular practice works better than occasional long sessions.

1. **Learn one technique at a time.** The [Linking Method](/tactics/linking-method) is the easiest place to start.
2. **Practise for five to ten minutes.** One or two rounds of a practice game is enough.
3. **Come back tomorrow.** The [daily challenge](/daily) takes about two minutes and builds a streak, which makes the habit stick.
4. **Raise the difficulty when it feels easy.** More items and less time force your images to become quicker and sharper.

The first few attempts can feel slow and awkward. That's normal: you're learning to think in a new way. Most people notice a real difference within a week.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do memory champions have a photographic memory?

No. Studies of top memory competitors found normal intelligence and normal brain structure. They rely on learned techniques like the memory palace, not on a special kind of memory.

### What is the picture superiority effect?

It's the finding that people remember pictures better than words. Memory techniques use it by converting words, names and numbers into vivid mental images.

### Can anyone learn to memorize like a champion?

The core techniques, yes. Reaching competition level takes a lot of practice, but most people see a big improvement within a few weeks of short daily training.
