**Short answer:** to remember someone's name, (1) actually pay attention when you hear it, (2) turn the name into a picture, (3) attach that picture to something distinctive about their face, and (4) use the name again within a few minutes. This is the **face-name technique**, and it works because it turns an abstract word into a vivid image tied to the person.

## Why do we forget names so quickly?

Most of the time we don't forget names: we never stored them in the first place. When you're introduced, you're busy thinking about what to say, shaking hands and making a first impression. The name passes through without being encoded.

Names are also hard on their own. "Kevin" means nothing visual, unlike "baker" or "pilot". Researchers call this the *Baker/baker paradox*: people remember that someone is a baker (a job they can picture) far better than that their name is Baker. The technique below fixes exactly that.

## Step 1: Catch the name

Decide to hear it. When someone says their name, pause and repeat it back: "Sofia, nice to meet you." If you didn't catch it, ask again straight away. It's far less awkward now than in ten minutes.

## Step 2: Turn the name into a picture

Some names are already pictures: Rose, Bill, Lily, Jack. For the rest, use a word that sounds similar:

- **Sofia** → sofa
- **Greg** → keg
- **Kevin** → cave
- **Mark** → a marker pen
- **Daniel** → a lion's den
- **Leo** → a roaring lion

It doesn't need to be clever or polite (nobody will ever know). It just needs to be easy to picture.

## Step 3: Link the picture to their face

Pick one feature that stands out: big curly hair, round glasses, a beard, a strong jaw, bright eyes. Then make the name picture *interact* with that feature, in an exaggerated way:

- Sofia has long wavy hair → a tiny **sofa** is riding the waves of her hair.
- Greg has broad shoulders → a beer **keg** is balanced on them.
- Mark has thick eyebrows → someone has drawn them on with a giant **marker**.

Next time you see the face, the feature pulls up the picture, and the picture gives you the name.

## Step 4: Use it again, soon

Say the name in conversation within a few minutes ("What do you think, Greg?") and once more when you say goodbye. Then, after the event, run through the faces you met and recall each name. That review is what moves names into long-term memory.

## Common mistakes

- **Choosing a feature that changes.** A red jumper won't be there next week; a nose, eyebrows or hairline will.
- **Making the image too subtle.** "Sofia sits on a sofa" is weak. "A sofa surfing on her hair" sticks.
- **Skipping the review.** Even a 30-second recap on the way home makes a big difference.

## Practice it

Like any skill, this gets fast with practice. The [Face-Name practice game](/tactics/face-name/practice) shows you faces with names and distinctive features, then tests you, with instant feedback on every answer.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is being bad with names a sign of poor memory?

No. Names are one of the hardest things for anyone to remember, because they carry no meaning or image on their own. Almost everyone struggles with them until they learn a technique.

### What if the name has no obvious picture?

Use a word that sounds similar, even loosely: Kevin → cave, Priya → pliers, Omar → oh, ma! The sound-alike only has to remind you of the name, not match it exactly.

### How do memory champions remember so many names?

They use the same steps: a strong image for each name, linked to a distinctive facial feature, created quickly. Competitions include a names-and-faces event, and the winners rely on this method plus a lot of practice.

### How can I remember names at a networking event?

Repeat each name when you hear it, create the image right away, use the name once in conversation, and review the faces and names on your way home. Writing them down afterwards also helps.
