The memory palace, also called the **Method of Loci**, is more than two thousand years old. Ancient Greek and Roman speakers used it to deliver long speeches without notes. Today it's the backbone of almost every memory competition: athletes use it to memorize decks of cards, long numbers and hundreds of words.

The good news is that you already own a memory palace. You just haven't used it yet.

## The idea in one sentence

You remember a list by placing each item, as a vivid image, at a specific spot along a route you know well, then walking that route in your mind to collect them again.

It works because humans are extremely good at remembering *places*. You can probably picture every room of your home right now, in order, without effort. The memory palace borrows that strength and puts it to work on information your brain finds boring.

## Step 1: Pick your palace

Choose a place you know so well you could walk through it with your eyes closed. Your home is the classic choice. Other good options:

- the route from your front door to your workplace
- your school or office building
- a friend's or family member's house

## Step 2: Plan a route with fixed stops

Pick 10 distinct spots (memory athletes call them *loci*) and always visit them in the same order. For example:

1. Front door
2. Coat hooks
3. Hallway mirror
4. Living room sofa
5. TV
6. Kitchen table
7. Fridge
8. Sink
9. Bathroom bathtub
10. Your bed

Walk the route in your head a few times until it feels automatic. Each spot should be clearly different from the others: two identical chairs make poor neighbours.

## Step 3: Place the items

Now take your list and put one item at each stop, as a strange, active image. Say you need to remember: **eggs, passport, birthday card, batteries, dentist**.

- **Front door:** eggs are smashed all over it, dripping down onto the doormat.
- **Coat hooks:** a giant passport hangs there like a coat, flapping its pages.
- **Hallway mirror:** a birthday card is singing loudly from inside the glass.
- **Sofa:** batteries are bouncing on the cushions, sparking every time they land.
- **TV:** your dentist leans out of the screen holding a drill.

The rules from the [Linking Method](/blog/linking-method-beginners-guide) apply here too: be absurd, add action, exaggerate, and involve as many senses as you can.

## Step 4: Walk it back

To recall the list, walk your route again in your mind. Stop at the front door. What's there? Eggs, dripping everywhere. Coat hooks? The flapping passport. The places act as hooks, so you don't need to remember the list itself, only where you put things.

A big advantage over linking: if you forget one item, you don't lose the rest. Just move on to the next stop.

## Tips for getting better

- **Start small.** Five to ten loci is plenty for a first palace.
- **Review once soon after.** A quick walk-through a few minutes later strengthens the images a lot.
- **Build more palaces.** Once one works, add a second (your workplace, a relative's home). Competitors keep dozens.
- **Reuse with care.** You can reuse a palace for new lists, but give the old images a day or two to fade first.

## Practice it now

The [Method of Loci practice game](/tactics/memory-palace/practice) gives you a ready-made house with rooms to place words in, then asks you to recall what you left in each room. Start on Beginner with five locations, then work your way up.
